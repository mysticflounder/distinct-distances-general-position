\begin{abstract}
Place $n$ points in the plane so that no three are on a line and no four
are on a circle. How many different distances must they determine? Every
circle centred at one of the points can pass through at most three of the
others, so the $n-1$ other points need at least $(n-1)/3$ circles, and
there are at least $(n-1)/3$ distinct distances. I prove that every such set with $n \ge 10^4$ points
determines more than $(1/3 + 1/40)\,n$ distinct distances. The proof first
shows that a set with few distances must lie in a thin ring (an annulus)
around its centre of mass. It then derives many linear inequalities that
the distribution of the pairwise distances must satisfy, and shows that no
distribution satisfies all of them. The last step is an explicit
certificate: nonnegative rational weights such that the weighted sum of the inequalities is false for every possible profile. The certificate has been
checked by an independent exact verifier, and the complete proof, with the
certificate, has been checked by the Lean~4 proof assistant.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The question}

For a finite set $P$ of points in the plane, let $D(P)$ be the number of
\emph{distinct} distances between pairs of its points: we list the distance
$|pq|$ for every pair $p \ne q$ and count how many different values occur.
A set of $n$ points has $\binom n2$ pairs, so $D(P) \le \binom n2$, and
for a typical set equality holds. The interesting question goes the other
way: how \emph{small} can $D(P)$ be? This is the distinct distances problem
of Erd\H{o}s.

The answer depends on what the set is allowed to look like.
Figure~\ref{fig:examples} shows three sets of $16$ points. The square grid
repeats each distance many times and has only $9$ distinct distances. The
vertices of a regular polygon have only $8$. A set with no special
structure has $\binom{16}{2} = 120$. Both small examples rely on structure:
the grid has many points on a common line, and the polygon has all its
points on a common circle.

\centering
\includegraphics{figures/fig_examples.pdf}
\caption{Three sets of $16$ points and their numbers of distinct distances.
(a)~The $4 \times 4$ grid has $D = 9$; it contains three points on a line
(drawn). (b)~The regular $16$-gon has $D = 8$; all its points lie on one
circle. (c)~$16$ points in general position: no three on a line, no four
on a circle. Here every distance occurs once, so $D = 120$.}
\label{fig:examples}
\end{figure}

We say that $P$ is \emph{in general position} if no three points of $P$
are collinear and no four points of $P$ are concyclic (on one circle).
Erd\H{o}s asked whether every set of $n$ points in general position
determines a number of distinct distances that grows faster than linearly
in $n$ \cite[question~(9)]{E75}. This is Problem~98 on the Erd\H{o}s
problems website \cite{EPweb}, where it is listed as open.

\subsection{The easy bound \texorpdfstring{$n/3$}{n/3}}

Fix a point $p \in P$ and draw, for every distance $r$ that occurs in $P$,
the circle with centre $p$ and radius $r$ (Figure~\ref{fig:circles}).
Every other point $q$ of $P$ lies on exactly one of these circles, the one
of radius $|pq|$. If four points of $P$ were on one of these circles, they
would be concyclic. So each circle holds at most three points of $P$, and
the $n-1$ other points need at least $(n-1)/3$ circles:
\[
  D(P) \;\ge\; \frac{n-1}{3}.
\]
Brass, Moser and Pach record this bound and state that no improvement of
the form $(1/3+\eps)n$ is known, both for sets with no four points on a
circle and for sets in general position \cite[\S5.5]{BMP05}. For sets with
no three points collinear, Erd\H{o}s reports an argument of Szemer\'edi
that gives a point with more than $\lfloor n/3\rfloor$ distances from it
\cite{E75}. I am not aware of a bound of the form $(1/3+\eps)n$ for sets
in general position. In the other direction, Brass, Moser and Pach list
the upper bound $n e^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}$ for such sets, due to Erd\H{o}s,
F\"uredi, Pach and Ruzsa \cite{EFPR93}. Without the general position
hypothesis the minimum lies between $cn/\log n$, by the theorem of Guth and
Katz \cite{GK}, and $Cn/\sqrt{\log n}$, attained by the square lattice.

\subsection{The result}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $P \subset \R^2$ be a set of $n \ge 10^4$ points, no three collinear and
no four concyclic. Then
\[
  D(P) \;>\; \Bigl(\frac13 + \frac1{40}\Bigr)\, n .
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is by contradiction: it assumes a set of $n \ge 10^4$ points in
general position with $D(P) \le (1/3+1/40)n$ and derives a contradiction.
The constant $1/40$ and the threshold $10^4$ are explicit, and both enter
through the certificate of Section~\ref{sec:cert}.

\subsection{How the proof works}\label{sec:overview}

This section explains the idea of the proof without formulas.
Figure~\ref{fig:roadmap} shows its structure.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/fig_roadmap.pdf}
\caption{The structure of the proof. Each box names the section where the
step is proved.}
\label{fig:roadmap}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Step 1: few distances leave few empty slots.} Think of every
distance $r$ in the set as providing three ``slots'' on the circle of
radius $r$ around $p$. The $n-1$ other points fill $n-1$ of the $3D$
slots, so $Q = 3D - (n-1)$ slots around $p$ stay empty. This number is the
same for every point $p$. If $D$ is at most $(1/3+\delta)n$, then
$Q \le 3\delta n + 1$: only a small fraction of the slots are empty. With $\delta = 1/40$, at every point at least about $79\%$ of the $D$ circles hold exactly three points. This is a rigid structure, and the rest of the proof shows that it cannot exist. One consequence (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) concerns isosceles triangles: at least about $85\%$ of the pairs of points $\{a,b\}$ are the base of
exactly two isosceles triangles whose apex is a point of the set.

\paragraph{Step 2: the set lies in a thin ring.} The sum of the squared
distances from a point $p$ to the other points measures how far $p$ is from
the rest of the set. A classical identity (the parallel axis theorem of
mechanics) says that this sum equals $n|p|^2 + S$, where $|p|$ is the
distance from $p$ to the centroid (centre of mass) of the set and $S$ does
not depend on $p$. So points far from the centroid have larger sums. On the
other hand, if all slots around $p$ are full, the sum is $3\sum_r r^2$,
summed over all the distances $r$ of the set, which also does not depend on
$p$. So if there were no empty slots at all, every point would be at the
same distance from the centroid: the whole set would lie on one circle,
which general position forbids. With few empty slots, the points are
nearly at the same distance from the centroid: after scaling, they lie in
the \emph{annulus}
\[
  0.836 \;\le\; |p| \;\le\; 1,
\]
a ring of width about $0.16$ around the centroid. Section~\ref{sec:annulus}
proves this, and Figures~\ref{fig:distance-lists} and~\ref{fig:annulus}
illustrate it. The annulus is the main tool of the proof: a circle of medium radius around a point of the ring meets the ring in two short arcs, and a line that passes near the centre meets the ring in two short segments. This gives many geometric
restrictions.

\paragraph{Step 3: inequalities for the distance profile.} The proof does
not work with individual points. It works with the \emph{distance profile}:
for each $\rho$ between $0$ and $2$, the fraction $\cA(\rho)$ of pairs of
points at distance at most $\rho$, and the number of distinct distances at
most $\rho$. These two functions are like cumulative distribution
functions. Each geometric fact about the ring gives an inequality between
values of these functions. Some examples: a full circle of medium radius
around a point of the ring must contain two close points
(Section~\ref{sec:profile}); a long pair must have its two apexes at
distances in a known range (Section~\ref{sec:rows}); and the two apexes of
a long pair are either very close to each other or very far apart
(Section~\ref{sec:J}). All these inequalities are linear in the unknown
values of the profile.

\paragraph{Step 4: no profile satisfies all the inequalities.} We now have
a system of linear inequalities in finitely many unknowns, and we want to
show that it has no solution. There is a simple way to prove that a system
of linear inequalities has no solution: multiply each inequality by a
nonnegative number and add them up. If the result is a false statement such
as $0 \le -1$, the system has no solution, because any solution would also
satisfy the sum. Figure~\ref{fig:farkas} shows a small example. The list of
multipliers is called a \emph{certificate}. A computer finds the
multipliers for our system, but the computer is not needed to trust the
result: once the multipliers are written down, anyone can check them with
exact arithmetic on fractions. Section~\ref{sec:cert} describes the
certificate, and Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes two checks of it: an exact verifier that does not use the program that found the multipliers, and a formal proof in the Lean proof assistant.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/fig_farkas.pdf}
\caption{A certificate in a small example. The three inequalities
$x \ge 1$, $y \ge 1$ and $x + y \le 1$ have no common solution: the three

\section{Empty slots and isosceles triangles}\label{sec:setup}

Throughout, $P \subset \R^2$ is a set of $n \ge 2$ points in general
position, $R$ is the set of distances determined by $P$, and $D = |R|$.
For $p \in P$ and $r > 0$ let $C(p,r)$ be the circle with centre $p$ and
radius $r$, and
\[
  d_r(p) = |P \cap C(p,r)|, \qquad \operatorname{def}_r(p) = 3 - d_r(p) .
\]
So $d_r(p)$ is the number of points of $P$ at distance exactly $r$ from
$p$, and $\operatorname{def}_r(p)$ is the number of empty slots on that
circle. Four points of $P$ on $C(p,r)$ would be concyclic, so
$d_r(p) \le 3$ and $\operatorname{def}_r(p) \ge 0$. Every
$q \in P \setminus \{p\}$ lies on exactly one circle $C(p,r)$ with
$r \in R$, so $\sum_{r \in R} d_r(p) = n-1$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
  \sum_{r \in R} \operatorname{def}_r(p) \;=\; 3D - n + 1 \;=:\; Q
  \qquad \text{for every } p \in P .
\end{equation}
In particular $Q \ge 0$, which is the bound $D \ge (n-1)/3$. We call $Q$
the \emph{deficit}. It is the number of empty slots around each point,
and it is the same for all points.

\paragraph{Isosceles triangles.} For distinct $a, b \in P$ let
\[
  \alpha(a,b) = \#\{z \in P\setminus\{a,b\} : |za| = |zb|\},
\]
the number of points $z$ of $P$ that are the apex of an isosceles triangle
with base $ab$. Such apexes lie on the perpendicular bisector
$\bis(a,b)$, which is a line, and no three points of $P$ are collinear, so
$\alpha(a,b) \le 2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:isosceles}(a)). Let $N = \binom n2$
be the number of unordered pairs and let $x_k$ be the number of unordered
pairs with $\alpha = k$, so $N = x_0 + x_1 + x_2$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/fig_isosceles.pdf}
\caption{(a)~The apexes $z, z'$ of the isosceles triangles with base $ab$
lie on the perpendicular bisector of $ab$. A third apex would put three

(Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa}). If the pairs $f = \{a, \sigma a\}$ and $g = \{b, \sigma b\}$ both have the apexes $z, z'$, each pair is symmetric about the line $\ell = zz'$,
and the point $c$ where the bisector of $ab$ meets $\ell$ is equidistant
from all four points: four points on a circle.}
\label{fig:isosceles}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What the next lemma says.} The total number of ``missing
apexes'', $\sum (2 - \alpha)$ over all pairs, is at most $n$ times the
number $Q$ of empty slots per point. When $D \le (1/3+\delta)n$, this says
that at most about $3\delta n^2$ pairs have fewer than two apexes; with $\delta = 1/40$
that is at most about $15\%$ of the $N \approx n^2/2$ pairs. The idea of
the proof: a point $z$ is an apex of the pair $\{a,b\}$ exactly when $a$
and $b$ lie on a common circle around $z$, and a full circle with three
points supplies three such pairs.

\begin{lemma}[apex identity]\label{lem:apex}
Let $V = \sum_{p \in P}\sum_{r \in R} v(d_r(p))$ with $v(0) = 3$,
$v(1) = 1$, $v(2) = v(3) = 0$. Then
\[
  \sum_{\{a,b\}} \bigl(2 - \alpha(a,b)\bigr) + V \;=\; nQ ,
\]
the sum running over the $N$ unordered pairs. Consequently
$N - x_2 \le nQ$, and for every set $\Phi$ of ordered pairs of distinct
points that is closed under $(a,b) \mapsto (b,a)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:B2}
  \sum_{(a,b) \in \Phi} \bigl(2 - \alpha(a,b)\bigr) \;\le\; 2nQ .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Count the pairs (apex $z$, unordered pair $\{a,b\} \subset C(z,r)$): a
circle $C(z,r)$ with $d$ points gives $\binom d2$ pairs, so
$\sum_{\{a,b\}} \alpha(a,b) = \sum_{z}\sum_{r\in R}\binom{d_r(z)}{2}$.
Also $2N = \sum_z \sum_{r \in R} d_r(z)$, because each point $z$ has $n-1$
partners. With \eqref{eq:Q},
\[
  nQ - \sum_{\{a,b\}}(2-\alpha) = \sum_z \sum_{r \in R}
  \Bigl[(3 - d) - d + \tbinom d2\Bigr]_{d = d_r(z)},
\]
and $3 - 2d + \binom d2$ equals $3, 1, 0, 0$ for $d = 0, 1, 2, 3$. For the
consequences: $2 - \alpha \ge 0$ and $V \ge 0$, so
$N - x_2 = x_0 + x_1 \le 2x_0 + x_1 = \sum (2 - \alpha) \le nQ$; and the
sum in \eqref{eq:B2} is at most the sum over all ordered pairs, which is
$2\sum_{\{a,b\}}(2-\alpha) \le 2nQ$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The map $\kappa$.} Let $\cD$ be the set of unordered pairs $f$
with $\alpha(f) = 2$, so $|\cD| = x_2$. For $f \in \cD$ let $\kappa(f)$ be
the pair of points of $P$ on $\bis(f)$, that is, the pair of its two
apexes. The next lemma says that the two apexes determine the pair. It is
used in Section~\ref{sec:J}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kappa}
$\kappa$ is injective on $\cD$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $f \ne g \in \cD$ with $\kappa(f) = \kappa(g) = \{z, z'\}$, and let
$\ell$ be the line $zz'$ (Figure~\ref{fig:isosceles}(b)). Then
$\bis(f) = \bis(g) = \ell$, so with $\sigma$ the reflection in $\ell$ we
have $f = \{a, \sigma a\}$ and $g = \{b, \sigma b\}$. If $f$ and $g$ share
a point, then $f = g$; so they are disjoint. If $a$ and $b$ lie on one
line perpendicular to $\ell$, the four points $a, \sigma a, b, \sigma b$
are collinear, which general position excludes. Otherwise $\bis(a,b)$ is
not parallel to $\ell$ and meets $\ell$ at a point $c$, and
$|ca| = |cb| = |c\sigma a| = |c\sigma b|$: four points of $P$ on a circle,
which general position also excludes.
\end{proof}

\section{The annulus}\label{sec:annulus}

This section proves Step~2 of the overview: a set with few distinct
distances lies in a thin ring around its centroid. Everything here uses
only the two expressions \eqref{eq:parallel} and \eqref{eq:radial} below
for one sum, and the count \eqref{eq:Q}.

Translate $P$ so that its centroid is the origin, $\sum_{p} p = 0$, and
put $S = \sum_p |p|^2$. Translation does not change distances, collinearity
or concyclicity.

\paragraph{Two ways to compute the same sum.} For a point $p$, consider the
sum $\sum_{q \in P} |p-q|^2$ of the squared distances from $p$ to all
points of $P$. On one hand, expanding $|p - q|^2 = |p|^2 - 2\langle p, q
\rangle + |q|^2$ and using $\sum_q q = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parallel}
  \sum_{q \in P} |p-q|^2 \;=\; n|p|^2 + S .
\end{equation}
(This is the parallel axis theorem: the moment of inertia about $p$ is the
moment about the centroid plus $n$ times the squared distance from the
centroid.) So the sum grows with the distance $|p|$ from $p$ to the
centroid. On the other hand, grouping the points $q$ by their distance $r$
from $p$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radial}
  \sum_{q \in P} |p-q|^2 \;=\; \sum_{r \in R} d_r(p)\, r^2
  \;=\; 3\sum_{r \in R} r^2 \;-\; \sum_{r \in R} \operatorname{def}_r(p)\, r^2 .
\end{equation}
The first term on the right, $3\sum_r r^2$, is the same for every point
$p$: it would be the value of the sum if every circle around $p$ were full.
The second term is the ``missing moment'' of the empty slots at $p$. If
there were no empty slots, \eqref{eq:parallel} and \eqref{eq:radial} would
give the same $|p|$ for every $p$, so all points would lie on one circle
around the centroid. If there are few empty slots, the missing moment is
small, and all points are \emph{nearly} at the same distance from the
centroid. Figure~\ref{fig:distance-lists} shows the effect in random
examples. The lemmas below make this precise.

\centering

If $D$ is close to $n/3$, every point uses most distances of the set exactly three times, so all points see nearly the same list, and by \eqref{eq:parallel} and \eqref{eq:radial} they are nearly at the same distance from the centroid. (Random sets with
$3000$ points; they are illustrations, not sets with few distances.)}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rho}
$\rho_A^2 := \bigl(3\sum_{r \in R} r^2 - S\bigr)/n$ is positive.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Summing \eqref{eq:parallel} over $p$ gives $\sum_{p,q}|p-q|^2 = 2nS$. By
\eqref{eq:radial} and $d_r(p) \le 3$,
$\sum_q |p-q|^2 \le 3\sum_{r\in R} r^2$. Summing over $p$ gives
$2nS \le 3n\sum_r r^2$, so $3\sum_r r^2 - S \ge S$, and $S > 0$ because
$P$ has at least two points and centroid $0$.
\end{proof}

Scale $P$ by $1/\rho_A$. Scaling preserves general position and $D$, and
after it
\begin{equation}\tag{N1}
  3\sum_{r\in R} r^2 = n + S .
\end{equation}
For $p \in P$ put
\[
  m_p = \frac1n\sum_{r \in R}\operatorname{def}_r(p)\,r^2 \;\ge\; 0,
\]
the missing moment of $p$, divided by $n$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:annulus}
(N2) $|p|^2 = 1 - m_p$ for every $p \in P$. (N3) $|p| \le 1$, every
distance in $P$ is at most $2$, and $R \subset (0,2]$. (N4) If
$D \le (1/3+\delta)n$, then $m_p \le 12\delta + 4/n$ for every $p$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(N2): by \eqref{eq:parallel}, \eqref{eq:radial} and (N1),
$n|p|^2 + S = 3\sum_r r^2 - n m_p = n + S - n m_p$. (N3) follows from
$m_p \ge 0$: all points are in the unit disk, so any two are at distance
at most $2$. (N4): by (N3) every $r \in R$ satisfies $r^2 \le 4$, so
$m_p \le 4Q/n$ by \eqref{eq:Q}, and $Q = 3D - n + 1 \le 3\delta n + 1$.
\end{proof}

So after the normalization, the outer boundary of the ring is the unit
circle, and the depth $m_p$ of a point below the unit circle (measured in
$|p|^2$) is controlled by its empty slots. A point with all slots full
would be on the unit circle. The general position hypothesis enters here
only through $d_r(p) \le 3$.

\paragraph{The instance.} From now on assume, for a contradiction, that
$n \ge n_0 = 10^4$ and $D \le (1/3 + \delta)n$ with $\delta = 1/40$. Put
$\bar m = 12\delta + 4/n_0 = 3/10 + 4/10^4$ and
\[
  \sbar = \frac{41821}{50000} = 0.83642, \qquad
  \sbar^2 = \frac{1748996041}{2500000000} \le 1 - \bar m , \qquad
  \omega := 1 - \sbar^2 \approx 0.3004 .
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus}, every point of $P$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:annulus}
  \sbar^2 \le |p|^2 \le 1 .
\end{equation}
This is \emph{the annulus}: the ring between the circles of radius
$\sbar \approx 0.836$ and $1$ around the centroid
(Figure~\ref{fig:annulus}(a)). The rational number $\sbar$ is chosen
slightly below $\sqrt{1 - \bar m}$ so that all later computations are with
exact fractions. For a smaller $\delta$ the ring is thinner
(Figure~\ref{fig:annulus}(b)).

For $p \in P$ put $y_p = |p|^2 - \sbar^2 \in [0,\omega]$, the height of
$p$ above the inner boundary (measured in $|p|^2$), and let
$\ybar = n^{-1}\sum_p y_p$ be its average. The final step of the proof
splits into cases according to the value of $\ybar$.

four points on a circle.}
\label{fig:annulus}
\end{figure}

\section{The distance profile and short chords}\label{sec:profile}

\paragraph{The unknowns.} For $\rho \ge 0$ let
$N_p(\rho) = \#\{q \in P : |pq| \le \rho\}$ (with $p$ included) and
$R(\rho) = \#\{r \in R : r \le \rho\}$. Put
\[
  \dd = \frac Dn, \quad \eps = \frac1n, \quad \qq = \frac Qn = 3\dd - 1 + \eps,
  \quad \cR(\rho) = \frac{R(\rho)}n, \quad
  \cA(\rho) = \frac1{n^2}\sum_p \bigl(N_p(\rho) - 1\bigr),
\]
and $\cX(\rho) = n^{-2}\sum_p\sum_{r\in R,\, r\le\rho}\operatorname{def}_r(p)$.
In words:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\cA(\rho)$ is the fraction of ordered pairs at distance at most
  $\rho$: $n^2\cA(\rho)$ is the number of ordered pairs of distinct points
  at distance at most $\rho$, and the number of unordered pairs $e$ with
  $|e| \le \rho$ is $n^2\cA(\rho)/2$. It is a cumulative distribution
  function of the pair distances, up to the factor $1 - \eps$.
\item $\cR(\rho)$ is the number of distinct distances up to $\rho$,
  divided by $n$. So $3\cR(\rho)$ is the number of slots per point on
  circles of radius at most $\rho$, divided by $n$.
\item $\cX(\rho)$ is the number of empty slots on circles of radius at most $\rho$, summed over all points and divided by $n^2$. Since
  $\sum_{r\le\rho} d_r(p) = N_p(\rho) - 1$ (filled slots), we have
  $\cX = 3\cR - \cA$.
\item $\dd$, $\eps$ and $\qq$ are the number of distances, $1$ and the
  deficit, all divided by $n$.
\end{itemize}
The proof treats the values of these functions at finitely many radii as
the unknowns of a linear system. Figure~\ref{fig:profile} in
Section~\ref{sec:remarks} shows what a solution of that system looks like
when $\delta$ is slightly larger than $1/40$.

\paragraph{Facts.} (F1) $\cR$, $\cA$ and $\cX$ are nondecreasing,
$0 \le \cX \le \qq$ and $\cR \le \dd$. (F2) $\cR(2) = \dd$ and
$\cA(2) = 1 - \eps$, by (N3). (F3) $1/3 - 1/(3n_0) \le \dd \le 1/3 + \delta$
and $0 < \eps \le 1/n_0$.

\paragraph{Short chords.} The first geometric consequence of the annulus
concerns a circle $C(p,r)$ around a point $p$ of the ring. If the radius $r$ is neither very small nor very large, the circle leaves the ring and comes back, and the part of it inside the ring consists of two arcs, mirror images of each other in the line through $0$ and $p$ (Figure~\ref{fig:chord}). (A very small circle can lie entirely inside the ring; then the lemma below gives no useful bound, and the certificate uses the lemma only for radii where it does.) If the circle holds three points of $P$, two of
them are on the same arc, so they are close to each other. The next lemma computes how close. In it, $\varphi$ is the angle at $p$, measured from the direction that points away from $0$; $L$ and $L + w$ are the values of $\cos\varphi$ at which $C(p,r)$ crosses the inner and the outer boundary circle of the annulus; $\cl$ clamps a number to $[-1,1]$; and $f$ is the angle that one arc spans, seen from $p$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/fig_chord.pdf}
\caption{Lemma~\ref{lem:C}. (a)~A circle $C(p,r)$ of medium radius around a point $p$ of the annulus meets the annulus in two arcs (thick), mirror images in the
line through the centroid $0$ and $p$. Seen from $p$, each arc spans the
angle $f$. Of three points $q_1, q_2, q_3$ of $P$ on the circle, two lie
on the same arc. (b)~A zoom on the upper arc: $q_1$ and $q_2$ are joined by
a short chord. Here $|p| = 0.92$, $r = 0.8$ and $f \approx 13^\circ$.}
\label{fig:chord}
\end{figure}

For $\tau > 0$ and an interval $I$ let $C(I,\tau)$ be the statement: for
every $p \in P$ and every $r \in R\cap I$ with $d_r(p) = 3$, two of the
three points of $P$ on $C(p,r)$ are at distance at most $\tau$.

\begin{lemma}[same-side chord]\label{lem:C}
Let $p \in P$, $a = |p|$, $r > 0$,
$L = (\sbar^2 - a^2 - r^2)/(2ar)$, $w = (1-\sbar^2)/(2ar)$, let $\cl$ be
the clamp to $[-1,1]$ and $f = \arccos(\cl L) - \arccos(\cl(L+w)) \in
[0,\pi]$. Then any three points of $P$ on $C(p,r)$ contain two at squared
distance at most $2r^2(1 - \cos f)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Rotate so that $p = (a,0)$ and write $q = p + r(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$.
Then $|q|^2 = a^2 + r^2 + 2ar\cos\varphi$, so by \eqref{eq:annulus} a point
$q \in P\cap C(p,r)$ has $\cos\varphi \in [L, L+w]$. Two of three points
lie in the same closed half $\varphi \in [0,\pi]$ or $\varphi \in
[\pi,2\pi]$; reflecting both in the first axis if needed (this preserves
their distance), both lie in $[0,\pi]$. Since $\cos$ is a decreasing
bijection $[0,\pi] \to [-1,1]$, both angles lie in
$[\arccos \cl(L+w), \arccos \cl L]$, so they differ by at most $f$, and the
two points are at distance $2r\sin(|\Delta\varphi|/2) \le 2r\sin(f/2)$.
\end{proof}

The bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:C} depends on $a = |p|$ and $r$. A computer
can only check finitely many cases, so the next lemma gives one bound for
a whole box of values $a \in [a_0, a_1]$, $r \in [r_0, r_1]$. Covering
the annulus and a range of radii by finitely many boxes then proves a
statement $C(I, \tau)$.

\begin{lemma}[box bound]\label{lem:Cbox}
Let $\sbar \le a_0 < a_1 \le 1$ and $0 < r_0 < r_1$. Put
$L_{\mathrm{lo}} = (\sbar^2 - a_1^2 - r_1^2)/(2a_0r_0)$,
$L_{\mathrm{hi}} = (\sbar^2 - a_0^2 - r_0^2)/(2a_1r_1)$,
$w_{\mathrm{hi}} = (1-\sbar^2)/(2a_0r_0)$,
\[
  \Lambda = \{L_{\mathrm{lo}}, L_{\mathrm{hi}}\} \cup
  \bigl(\{-1,\, 1 - w_{\mathrm{hi}}\} \cap [L_{\mathrm{lo}}, L_{\mathrm{hi}}]\bigr),
\]
and let $\beta \le \min_{L \in \Lambda}\bigl[\cl L\cdot \cl(L+w_{\mathrm{hi}})
+ \sqrt{(1 - (\cl L)^2)(1 - (\cl(L+w_{\mathrm{hi}}))^2)}\bigr]$. Then for every
$a \in [a_0,a_1]$ and $r \in [r_0, r_1]$ the bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:C} is at
most $2r_1^2(1-\beta)$. Also, for every $r \in (0, r_1]$, any two points of
$C(p,r)$ are at squared distance at most $4r_1^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The numerator $\sbar^2 - a^2 - r^2$ is negative and the denominator $2ar$ is
positive, so $L \in [L_{\mathrm{lo}}, L_{\mathrm{hi}}]$ and
$w \le w_{\mathrm{hi}}$. For fixed $L$, $f(L,w)$ is nondecreasing in $w$.
For fixed $w$, $f(\cdot,w)$ is continuous and is: $0$ if $L + w \le -1$;
increasing when $L < -1 < L + w \le 1$; on $-1 \le L$, $L + w \le 1$ its
derivative $-1/\sqrt{1-L^2} + 1/\sqrt{1-(L+w)^2}$ is $\le 0$ for
$L \le -w/2$ and $\ge 0$ for $L \ge -w/2$, so it decreases and then
increases; decreasing when $-1 \le L \le 1 < L+w$; $0$ if $L > 1$; and
$\pi$ on $[1-w, -1]$ when $w > 2$. When $w \le 2$ we have
$-1 \le -w/2 \le 1 - w$, so the only interior local maxima are at $-1$ and
$1-w$. Hence the maximum of $f(\cdot, w_{\mathrm{hi}})$ on
$[L_{\mathrm{lo}}, L_{\mathrm{hi}}]$ is attained at a point of $\Lambda$.
Finally $\cos(\arccos u - \arccos v) = uv + \sqrt{1-u^2}\sqrt{1-v^2}$, so
$\cos f \ge \beta$ on the box, and $2r^2(1-\cos f) \le 2r_1^2(1-\beta)$.
The last claim holds because $C(p,r)$ has diameter $2r$.
\end{proof}

In the certificate, every statement $C(I,\tau)$ that an inequality needs
is proved by covering $[\sbar, 1] \times \overline I$ with finitely many
boxes, on each of which Lemma~\ref{lem:Cbox} gives a bound at most
$\tau^2$, with square roots rounded so that $\beta$ is a lower bound; a
box with $r_0 = 0$ uses the bound $4r_1^2$. When no such cover exists for
an interval, the corresponding inequality is not used.

\section{Pair counts, apex ranges, a cap and a moment}\label{sec:rows}

This section derives four families of inequalities for the profile.

\paragraph{What the next lemma says.} Count, for each point $p$, the pairs
$\{q, q'\}$ of points on a common circle around $p$ that are at distance
at most $\rho$ from each other. A pair is counted once for each of its
apexes, so at most twice, and the total count is at most twice the number
of close pairs, which is $n^2\cA(\rho)$. On the other hand, a circle of radius at most $\rho/2$ has
diameter at most $\rho$, so all of its pairs are close; and a full circle
of radius in $(\rho/2, r']$ has a close pair by the chord statement.
Comparing the two counts gives lower bounds for $\cA(\rho)$, the fraction
of close pairs, in terms of the profile at other radii.

\begin{lemma}[pair counts]\label{lem:P}
Let $\rho > 0$ and $\rho/2 < r' \le 2$, and assume $C((\rho/2, r'], \rho)$.
Then
\begin{align*}
  &\text{(a)}\quad \cA(\rho) \ge \cA(r') - 2\cR(r'),\\
  &\text{(b)}\quad \cA(\rho) \ge \cA(\rho/2) + \cA(r') - \cR(\rho/2) - 2\cR(r'),\\
  &\text{(c)}\quad \cA(\rho) \ge \cA(r') - \cA(\rho/2) - 2\cR(r') + 2\cR(\rho/2).
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $I_p = \sum_{r \in R}\#\{\{q,q'\} \subset P\cap C(p,r) : |qq'| \le \rho\}$.
An unordered pair $\{q,q'\}$ with $|qq'| \le \rho$ is counted once for each
apex $p$, that is $\alpha(q,q') \le 2$ times, so
$\sum_p I_p \le 2\#\{\{q,q'\}: |qq'| \le \rho\} = n^2\cA(\rho)$. A radius
$r \le \rho/2$ contributes $\binom{d_r(p)}2$ to $I_p$, because all pairs on
$C(p,r)$ are within $2r \le \rho$. A radius $r \in (\rho/2, r']$ contributes
at least $[d_r(p) = 3] \ge d_r(p) - 2$ by the hypothesis, where $[d_r(p) = 3]$ is $1$ if $d_r(p) = 3$ and $0$ otherwise. For
$d \in \{0,1,2,3\}$, $\binom d2 \ge d - 2$, $\binom d2 \ge 2d-3$ and
$\binom d2 \ge 0$. Dropping the radii above $r'$ and using
$\sum_{r \le y} d_r(p) = N_p(y) - 1$, the three choices, used for the radii $r \le \rho/2$, give in this order, for each $p$,
$I_p \ge N_p(r') - 1 - 2R(r')$, $I_p \ge (N_p(\rho/2) - 1) + (N_p(r') - 1) -
R(\rho/2) - 2R(r')$, and $I_p \ge N_p(r') - N_p(\rho/2) - 2R(r') +
2R(\rho/2)$. Sum over $p$ and divide by $n^2$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What the next lemma says.} Take a long pair $\{a,b\}$, with
$|ab| > \lambda$, and an apex $z$ of it. All three points lie in the
annulus, and this restricts the distance $r = |za|$: its square lies
between two explicit numbers $\mathrm{lo}(\lambda)$ and
$\mathrm{hi}(\lambda)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:apex}, most long pairs have two
apexes. So the set must have many pairs $(z, a)$ with distance in the
allowed range, and this is a lower bound for the increase of $\cA$ over
that range.

\begin{lemma}[apex range]\label{lem:Apex}
Let $0 < \lambda < 2$ with $\lambda^2 \ge 4\omega$, put
$\mathrm{lo}(\lambda) = \sbar^2 + 1 - \sbar\sqrt{4-\lambda^2}$ and
$\mathrm{hi}(\lambda) = 2 + \sqrt{4 - \lambda^2}$, take
$0 < w^2 \le \mathrm{lo}(\lambda)$ and $\mathrm{hi}(\lambda) \le h^2 \le 4$,
and assume $C((w,2],\lambda)$. Then
\[
  2\bigl(1-\eps-\cA(\lambda)\bigr) - \tfrac43\bigl(\cA(h) - \cA(w)\bigr)
  \;\le\; 2\qq .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Let $z \notin \{a,b\}$ with $|za| = |zb| = r$ and $|ab| > \lambda$. Put
$u = |a|^2 + |b|^2$ and $\zeta = |z|$. The parallelogram law gives
$|a+b|^2 = 2u - |ab|^2 < 2u - \lambda^2$, and
$2r^2 = |z-a|^2 + |z-b|^2 = 2\zeta^2 + u - 2\langle z, a+b\rangle$. Hence
$\Psi^- < 2r^2 < \Psi^+$ with
$\Psi^\pm = 2\zeta^2 + u \pm 2\zeta\sqrt{2u - \lambda^2}$. By
\eqref{eq:annulus}, $\zeta \in [\sbar,1]$ and $u \in [2\sbar^2, 2]$. Then
$\partial_\zeta \Psi^- \ge 4\sbar - 2\sqrt{4 - \lambda^2} \ge 0$ and, at
$\zeta = \sbar$, $\partial_u\Psi^- = 1 - 2\sbar/\sqrt{2u-\lambda^2} \le 0$
for $u \in (\lambda^2/2, 2]$, both because $4\sbar^2 \ge 4 - \lambda^2$;
the actual $u$ lies in $(\lambda^2/2, 2]$ because $0 \le |a+b|^2 < 2u - \lambda^2$, and $\Psi^-$ is continuous on
$[\lambda^2/2, 2]$. So
$\Psi^- \ge \Psi^-(\sbar, 2) = 2\,\mathrm{lo}(\lambda)$, and
$\Psi^+ \le 4 + 2\sqrt{4-\lambda^2} = 2\,\mathrm{hi}(\lambda)$.

(ii) Let $\Phi$ be the set of ordered pairs $(a,b)$ with $|ab| > \lambda$;
$|\Phi| = n^2(1 - \eps - \cA(\lambda))$. Each apex $z$ of $(a,b) \in \Phi$
lies at a distance $r$ from $a$ with $\mathrm{lo} < r^2 < \mathrm{hi}$ by
(i), so $w < r \le h$. A circle $C(z,r)$ with $d$ points of $P$ carries at
most $\frac43 d$ ordered pairs of $\Phi$: none for $d \le 1$, two for
$d = 2$, and for $d = 3$ at most four, because $C((w,2],\lambda)$ gives a
pair within $\lambda$. Hence $\sum_\Phi \alpha \le \frac43\sum_z\#\{q :
w < |zq| \le h\} = \frac43 n^2(\cA(h) - \cA(w))$. Insert this into
\eqref{eq:B2}, $2|\Phi| - \sum_\Phi\alpha \le 2nQ$, and divide by $n^2$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What the next lemma says.} The centroid is at the origin, so
the points cannot all crowd on one side of it. Around each point $z$, a
weight that is large near $z$ and averages to $1$ over the set then shows
that not too many points are close to $z$. The result is an upper bound
for a weighted average of $\cA$ over the radii up to $\rho_e \approx 1.75$.

\begin{lemma}[averaged cap]\label{lem:K}
Let $\rho_e^2 = 2\sbar^2 + 2\sbar$. For $0 < g_1 < \dots < g_m \le \rho_e$,
$\sum_{i<m} \cA(g_i)(g_{i+1}^2 - g_i^2) \le 2\sbar$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $z \in P$, $\zeta = |z| \in [\sbar, 1]$, and put
$F(q) = 1 + \langle q, z/\zeta\rangle$. By (N3), $F \ge 0$ on $P$, and
$\sum_q F(q) = n$ because the centroid is $0$. Since
$\langle q, z/\zeta\rangle = (|q|^2 + \zeta^2 - |q-z|^2)/(2\zeta)$,
\eqref{eq:annulus} gives $F(q) \ge \varphi_\zeta(|q-z|)$ with
$\varphi_\zeta(t) = 1 + (\sbar^2 + \zeta^2 - t^2)/(2\zeta)$. Its
derivative in $\zeta$ is $\frac12 - (\sbar^2 - t^2)/(2\zeta^2) \ge 0$
for $\zeta \ge \sbar$, so $\varphi_\zeta \ge \varphi_{\sbar} =
(\rho_e^2 - t^2)/(2\sbar)$. Therefore
$n \ge \sum_q \max(0, \varphi_{\sbar}(|qz|)) =
\frac1{\sbar}\int_0^{\rho_e} t\,N_z(t)\,dt$. Summing over $z$,
$\int_0^{\rho_e} t\,(n^2\cA(t) + n)\,dt \le \sbar n^2$, so
$\int_0^{\rho_e} t\cA(t)\,dt \le \sbar$. Since $\cA$ is nondecreasing, the
left side of the claim is at most twice this integral.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What the next lemma says.} The average of $|pq|^2$ over all ordered pairs $(p,q)$ is $2S/n$ by \eqref{eq:parallel}, and by (N2) $S/n$, the average of $|p|^2$, is $1$ minus the average missing moment. Written in terms of the profile, this gives an exact identity
between $\cA$, $\cX$ and the deficit $\qq$. Since the profile is only known
at grid points, the proof uses it as two inequalities (lower and upper Riemann sums). The proof writes the average missing moment as the Stieltjes integral $\int_0^2 \rho^2\,d\cX(\rho)$, which here is the finite sum, over $r \in R$, of $r^2$ times the jump of $\cX$ at $r$, and then integrates by parts.

\begin{lemma}[second moment]\label{lem:M}
$\int_0^2 \rho\,\bigl(\cA(\rho) + 2\cX(\rho)\bigr)\,d\rho = 1 + 4\qq - 2\eps$.
The integrand is nondecreasing by (F1), so on a partition
$0 = g_0 < \dots < g_m = 2$ the sum
$\sum_i \frac12(g_{i+1}^2 - g_i^2)(\cA + 2\cX)(g_i)$ is at most
$1 + 4\qq - 2\eps$, and the same sum with $(\cA + 2\cX)(g_{i+1})$ is at
least $1 + 4\qq - 2\eps$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each ordered pair $(p,q)$, $p = q$ allowed,
$2 - |pq|^2/2 = \int_{|pq|}^2 \rho\,d\rho$ by (N3). Summing and dividing by
$n^2$: the left side becomes $\int_0^2 \rho(\cA + \eps)\,d\rho$, and the
right side becomes $2 - S/n = 1 + \mavg$ with $\mavg = n^{-1}\sum_p m_p$,
by $\sum_{p,q}|p-q|^2 = 2nS$ and (N2). Finally
$\mavg = \int_0^2 \rho^2\,d\cX(\rho) = 4\cX(2) - 2\int_0^2\rho\cX(\rho)\,d\rho$
with $\cX(2) = \qq$ by (N3) and \eqref{eq:Q}.
\end{proof}

\section{Apex bands}\label{sec:AB}

\paragraph{What this section does.} Lemma~\ref{lem:Apex} uses the worst
case over all positions in the annulus. This section keeps more
information. For an isosceles triangle $(z,a,b)$ with a long base $ab$,
the geometry of the annulus confines the apex distance $r = |za|$ to a range whose ends move linearly with the heights $y_a + y_b$ and $y_z$ of the three points above the inner boundary. Splitting the
triangles into bands by the value of $r$ and recording the total heights
in each band gives linear inequalities whose coefficients involve
$\ybar$, the average height. Part (AB5) is the only place where $\ybar$
multiplies an unknown; this is why the final step splits into cases by
$\ybar$.

Fix a rational $\lambda < 2$ such that $C((0,2],\lambda)$ holds. Let $\Phi$
be the set of ordered pairs with $|ab| > \lambda$ and
$T = \{(z,a,b) : (a,b) \in \Phi,\ z \notin\{a,b\},\ |za| = |zb|\}$, the
isosceles triangles with a long base. Fix
$e_0 < e_1 < \dots < e_m = 2$ with $e_0 \le \lambda/2$, let $T_j$ be the
triangles with $e_j < |za| \le e_{j+1}$, and put $W_j = |T_j|/n^2$,
$U_j = n^{-2}\sum_{T_j}(y_a + y_b)$, $V_j = n^{-2}\sum_{T_j} y_z$, and for
$t > 0$, $K^\pm(t) = (4 \pm t)\sbar^2 \pm (4\sbar^2 - \lambda^2)/t$. Here $t > 0$ is a free parameter, $x^+ = \max(x, 0)$, and $W_j$, $U_j$, $V_j$ depend on $\lambda$ and on the band edges.

\begin{lemma}[apex bands]\label{lem:AB}
\begin{itemize}
\item[(AB0)] $\ybar = \omega - 4\qq + 2\int_0^2 \rho\,\cX(\rho)\,d\rho$.
\item[(AB1)] $\sum_j W_j \ge 2(1 - \eps - \cA(\lambda)) - 2\qq$.
\item[(AB2)] $W_j \le \frac43\bigl(\cA(e_{j+1}) - \cA(e_j)\bigr)$.
\item[(AB3)] $(1 + 2/t)U_j + (2+t)V_j \ge (2e_j^2 - K^+(t))W_j$.
\item[(AB4)] $(2/t - 1)^+U_j + (t-2)^+V_j \ge (K^-(t) - 2e_{j+1}^2)W_j$.
\item[(AB5)] $\sum_j U_j \le 12\,\ybar\,(\dd - \cR(\lambda))$ and, for
  every $h \le \lambda/2$, $\sum_j V_j \le 4\,\ybar\,(\dd - \cR(h))$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every triangle of $T$ has $2|za| \ge |ab| > \lambda$ and $|za| \le 2$, so it
lies in exactly one band. (AB0): $n^{-1}\sum_p|p|^2 = 1 - \mavg$ by (N2),
and $\mavg = 4\qq - 2\int_0^2\rho\cX$ as in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:M}. (AB1): $|T| = \sum_\Phi\alpha \ge 2|\Phi| - 2nQ$ by
\eqref{eq:B2}. (AB2): fix $z$ and a radius $r$ in the band; $C(z,r)$
carries at most $\frac43 d_r(z)$ ordered pairs of $\Phi$, as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:Apex}(ii), now using $C((0,2],\lambda)$; sum over the radii
in the band and over $z$.

For (AB3) and (AB4) take $(z,a,b) \in T$, $r = |za|$, $\ell = |ab|$, and
write $|z|^2 = \sbar^2 + y_z$, $u = |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 2\sbar^2 + Y$ with
$Y = y_a + y_b$, and $\Theta = 2u - \lambda^2 = 4\sbar^2 - \lambda^2 + 2Y$.
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:Apex}, $2r^2 = 2|z|^2 + u - 2\langle z, a+b\rangle$ and
$|a+b|^2 = 2u - \ell^2 < \Theta$, so $\Theta > 0$ and, by
the Cauchy--Schwarz and AM--GM inequalities,
$|2\langle z,a+b\rangle| < 2\sqrt{|z|^2 \Theta} \le t|z|^2 + \Theta/t$.
Hence
\[
  K^-(t) + (1 - 2/t)Y + (2-t)y_z \;<\; 2r^2 \;<\; K^+(t) + (1+2/t)Y + (2+t)y_z .
\]
On $T_j$, $r > e_j$; summing the right inequality over $T_j$ gives (AB3).
On $T_j$, $r \le e_{j+1}$, so $(2/t - 1)Y + (t-2)y_z > K^-(t) - 2e_{j+1}^2$;
replace the coefficients by their positive parts ($Y, y_z \ge 0$) and sum,
which gives (AB4).

(AB5): $\Phi$ is symmetric, so $\sum_T (y_a + y_b) = 2\sum_{(a,b)\in\Phi}
\alpha(a,b)\,y_a \le 4\sum_a y_a M_a$ with $M_a = \#\{b : |ab| > \lambda\}
= \sum_{r > \lambda} d_r(a) \le 3(D - R(\lambda))$. The ordered pairs of
$\Phi$ with apex $z$ lie on circles $C(z,r)$ with $r > \lambda/2 \ge h$,
and by the count in (AB2) there are at most $\frac43\sum_{r>h} d_r(z) \le
4(D - R(h))$ of them. Multiply by $y_z$, sum, and divide by $n^2$.
\end{proof}

\section{The bisector length gap}\label{sec:J}

The inequalities so far use only the number $\alpha$ of apexes of each
pair. The next lemma uses where the apexes are.

\paragraph{What the next lemma says.} Take a long pair $f = \{a,b\}$. Both
endpoints lie in the thin annulus, so they are at nearly the same distance
from the centroid, and the perpendicular bisector of $ab$ passes close to
the centroid. A line through (or near) the centre of a ring crosses the
ring in two short segments on opposite sides of the centre
(Figure~\ref{fig:bisector}). The two apexes of $f$ are points of $P$, so
they lie in the ring, on these two segments. Either both are on the same
segment, and then they are close to each other, or they are on opposite
segments, and then they are far apart. Medium distances are impossible.
Since $\kappa$ is injective (Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa}), this gives: the
number of long pairs with two apexes is at most the number of short pairs
plus the number of very long pairs.

\begin{lemma}[bisector length gap]\label{lem:J}
Let $X \subset \R^2$ be finite, no three points collinear and no four
concyclic, with $\sbar^2 \le |p|^2 \le 1$ for all $p \in X$, where
$0 < \sbar < 1$, and let $\omega = 1 - \sbar^2$; define $\cD$, $\kappa$,
$N$ and $x_2$ for $X$ as in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. Fix $\lambda > 0$ with
$h_0 := \omega/(2\lambda) < \sbar$ and put
\[
  \sigma_0 = \sqrt{1 - h_0^2} - \sqrt{\sbar^2 - h_0^2}, \qquad
  \Lambda_0 = 2\sqrt{\sbar^2 - h_0^2} .
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] If $f \in \cD$ and $|f| \ge \lambda$, then $|\kappa(f)| \le \sigma_0$
  or $|\kappa(f)| \ge \Lambda_0$.
\item[(ii)] $\#\{e : \lambda \le |e| < \Lambda_0\} \le \#\{e : |e| \le \sigma_0\}
  + (N - x_2)$, where $e$ runs over unordered pairs.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

(No centroid condition is used; the lemma is applied to $X = P$.)

\begin{proof}
(i) For $f = \{a,b\}$, $\bis(f) = \{x : 2\langle x, b - a\rangle = |b|^2 -
|a|^2\}$ lies at distance $h = \bigl||b|^2 - |a|^2\bigr|/(2|ab|) \le
\omega/(2\lambda) = h_0 < \sbar$ from $0$. Write $\bis(f) = \{c + tu : t \in
\R\}$ with $c$ the foot of the perpendicular from $0$ and $|u| = 1$, so
$|c + tu|^2 = h^2 + t^2$. The two points $z = c + tu$, $z' = c + t'u$ of
$\kappa(f)$ satisfy $\sbar^2 \le h^2 + t^2, h^2 + t'^2 \le 1$, so
$\sqrt{\sbar^2 - h^2} \le |t|, |t'| \le \sqrt{1-h^2}$; in particular $t$ and
$t'$ are nonzero. If $tt' > 0$, then $|zz'| = \bigl||t| - |t'|\bigr| \le
\sigma(h) := \sqrt{1-h^2} - \sqrt{\sbar^2 - h^2}$. If $tt' < 0$, then
$|zz'| = |t| + |t'| \ge \Lambda(h) := 2\sqrt{\sbar^2 - h^2}$. On $[0,\sbar)$,
$\sigma'(h) = h\bigl(1/\sqrt{\sbar^2 - h^2} - 1/\sqrt{1 - h^2}\bigr) \ge 0$
and $\Lambda$ is decreasing, so $\sigma(h) \le \sigma_0$ and
$\Lambda(h) \ge \Lambda_0$.

(ii) Let $F = \{f \in \cD : |f| \ge \lambda\}$. By (i) and
Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa}, $|F| \le \#\{|e| \le \sigma_0\} + \#\{|e| \ge
\Lambda_0\}$. Every pair with $|e| \ge \lambda$ that is not in $F$ is
outside $\cD$, so $|F| \ge \#\{|e| \ge \lambda\} - (N - x_2)$. Subtracting
gives (ii). (If $\Lambda_0 \le \lambda$, the left side of (ii) is $0$.)
\end{proof}

In the language of the profile, Lemma~\ref{lem:J} becomes a linear
inequality:

\begin{corollary}[row J]\label{cor:J}
In the setting of Section~\ref{sec:annulus}, let $\lambda$ be as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:J}, and let $g_2 < \Lambda_0$ and $g_\sigma \ge \sigma_0$ with
$g_2 > \lambda$. Then $\cA(g_2) - \cA(\lambda) - \cA(g_\sigma) \le 2\qq$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\{\lambda < |e| \le g_2\} \subseteq \{\lambda \le |e| < \Lambda_0\}$ and
$\{|e| \le \sigma_0\} \subseteq \{|e| \le g_\sigma\}$. Apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:J}(ii) and $N - x_2 \le nQ$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:apex}), multiply
by $2/n^2$, and use $\#\{|e|\le\rho\} = n^2\cA(\rho)/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Corollary~\ref{cor:J} adds information to the other families. A closely
related system without it (the same families with $34$ thresholds and a
slightly different set of (AB5)-type rows, built at $\delta = 1/44$ and at
$\delta = 177/7832$) has exact rational feasible points, and these points
violate Corollary~\ref{cor:J} by about $0.2827$ and $0.3045$ (exact
rational evaluation). At $\delta = 1/40$ the system of
Section~\ref{sec:cert} without Corollary~\ref{cor:J} is feasible in
floating-point arithmetic.
\end{remark}

\section{The certificate}\label{sec:cert}

This section turns the lemmas into a finite system of linear inequalities
and explains how its infeasibility is certified.

\paragraph{Certificates of infeasibility.} Suppose a system of linear
inequalities $Av \le b$ (one inequality per row of the matrix $A$) has a
solution $v$, and let $y \ge 0$ be a vector of nonnegative multipliers, one
per row. Multiplying row $i$ by $y_i$ and adding gives
$(y^{\mathsf T}A)\,v \le y^{\mathsf T} b$. So if we find $y \ge 0$ for
which this combined inequality fails for every possible $v$, the system has
no solution. In the example of Figure~\ref{fig:farkas} the combined
inequality is $0 \le -1$. In our system the unknowns are also known to lie
in a box (each unknown between known bounds), and it is enough that the
combined inequality fails for every $v$ in the box. This is easy to check:
the minimum of a linear function over a box is attained coordinatewise.
Farkas' lemma says that a certificate exists whenever a system has no
solution, but the proof below does not use Farkas' lemma: it only uses the
certificate, and the easy argument above.

\paragraph{Why a grid and why cases.} The profile functions $\cA$ and
$\cR$ have infinitely many values, but the inequalities only need their
values at the finitely many radii of a grid $G$; the monotonicity facts
(F1) connect neighbouring grid points. Most inequalities are linear in
these values. The exception is (AB5), where the unknown $\ybar$ multiplies
the unknowns $\dd - \cR(\lambda)$ and $\dd - \cR(h)$. Fixing $\ybar$ to a
short interval and replacing it by the right endpoint of that interval
makes (AB5) linear, and (AB0) becomes two linear inequalities that put the
right side of (AB0) into the interval. So the proof splits the possible
values of $\ybar$ into $60$ intervals and gives a separate certificate for
each.

\paragraph{Grid.} Let $G$ be the set of rationals $b/2^j \ge 1/125$
($j \ge 0$), where $b$ runs over the $160$ numbers
$2\cdot 2^{-k/160}$ ($0 \le k < 160$) rounded to six decimals, the number
$\lceil\sqrt3\cdot 10^6\rceil/10^6$, and $2$. Then $|G| = 1283$.

\paragraph{Variables and box.} The unknowns are $\dd$, $\eps$, $\cR(g)$ and
$\cA(g)$ for $g \in G$, and the band quantities $W_j, U_j, V_j$ of
Section~\ref{sec:AB} for $32$ thresholds $\lambda$ (below). The true values
satisfy the box: (F3), $\cR(g) \in [0, 1/3 + \delta]$, $\cA(g) \in [0,1]$,
$W_j \in [0,2]$ and $U_j, V_j \in [0,4]$ (since $|T| = \sum_\Phi\alpha \le
2n^2$, $U_j \le 2\omega W_j$ and $V_j \le \omega W_j$).

\paragraph{Rows.} All rows are linear inequalities in these unknowns whose
coefficients are explicit rationals; each instance carries its parameters, and every side condition is checked in exact arithmetic. (Each row is stored with a \emph{tag} that names its family and its parameters, for example the radii $\rho$ and $r'$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:P}. Its \emph{side conditions} are the hypotheses of the lemma for these parameters, such as $C((\rho/2, r'], \rho)$. The \emph{chord table} is the list of the statements $C(I, \tau)$ that the box covers of Lemma~\ref{lem:Cbox} prove.)
\begin{enumerate}
\item (F1), (F2) on $G$: monotonicity of $\cR$, $\cA$ and $\cX = 3\cR -
  \cA$ between consecutive grid points, $\cX \ge 0$, $\cX \le \qq$,
  $\cR \le \dd$, $\cR(2) = \dd$, $\cA(2) = 1 - \eps$.
\item Lemma~\ref{lem:P} (a)--(c) for each $\rho \in G$ for which a grid
  point $r' > \rho/2$ with $C((\rho/2, r'], \rho)$ is certified by
  Lemma~\ref{lem:Cbox} ($579$ values of $\rho$; $r'$ is read from the
  certifier's chord table, and $r' = 2$ when the whole range is
  certified); (b) and (c) only when $\rho/2 \in G$.
\item Lemma~\ref{lem:Apex} for every $\lambda \in G$ with
  $4\omega \le \lambda^2 < 4$, $w$ the largest grid point with $w^2 \le
  \mathrm{lo}(\lambda)$ (rounded down) and $h$ the smallest grid point with
  $h^2 \ge \mathrm{hi}(\lambda)$ (rounded up), when $C((w,2],\lambda)$ is
  certified.
\item Lemma~\ref{lem:K} on the grid points up to $\rho_e$ (rounded down),
  and the lower and upper Riemann sums of Lemma~\ref{lem:M} on $G$.
\end{enumerate}
These are $9577$ rows. Then:
\begin{enumerate}\setcounter{enumi}{4}
\item Lemma~\ref{lem:AB}, (AB1)--(AB4), for $32$ thresholds
  $\lambda \in G \cap [1, 2\sbar + 1/20)$, each with $C((0,2],\lambda)$
  certified, chosen greedily upward from $1$ with consecutive thresholds at
  least $1/50$ apart; band edges $e_0 =$ the largest grid point
  $\le \lambda/2$ and $e_k =$ the largest grid point
  $\le \min(e_0 + k/50, 2)$, ending at $2$; and
  $t \in \{(1/50)(5/4)^k : 0 \le k < 25\}$, a row of (AB3) or (AB4) being
  used only when its coefficient of $W_j$ is positive. These are $15182$
  rows. (The certificate file also lists $64$ rows of a weaker form of
  (AB5) with weight zero.)
\item Corollary~\ref{cor:J} for every $\lambda \in G$ with $h_0 < \sbar$:
  $g_2$ is the largest grid point below a rational lower bound of
  $\Lambda_0$, $g_\sigma$ the smallest grid point at or above a rational
  upper bound of $\sigma_0$, and $g_2 > \lambda$. These are $515$ rows.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Case split.} $\ybar \in [0,\omega]$. Split $[0,\omega]$ into the
$60$ intervals $[Y_k, Y_{k+1}] = \omega\,[k/60, (k+1)/60]$. In case $k$
($\ybar \in [Y_k, Y_{k+1}]$) add, for each threshold $\lambda$ with
$h = $ the largest grid point $\le \lambda/2$,
\[
  \textstyle\sum_j U_j \le 12\,Y_{k+1}(\dd - \cR(\lambda)), \qquad
  \sum_j V_j \le 4\,Y_{k+1}(\dd - \cR(h)),
\]
which follow from (AB5) and $\dd - \cR \ge 0$, and the two rows
\[
  Y_k \le \omega - 4\qq + 2\,\mathrm{Up}, \qquad
  \omega - 4\qq + 2\,\mathrm{Lo} \le Y_{k+1},
\]
where $\mathrm{Up}$ and $\mathrm{Lo}$ are the upper and lower Riemann sums
of $\int_0^2\rho\cX$ on $G$ ($\mathrm{Up}$ includes the cell $[0,\min G]$
with the value $\cX(\min G)$, $\mathrm{Lo}$ omits it); they follow from
(AB0) because $\cX$ is nondecreasing and nonnegative. The variable $\ybar$
itself does not occur.

\paragraph{The certificate step.} Write the rows of case $k$ as $Av \le b$,
where $v$ is the vector of true values. For each $k$ the certificate gives
nonnegative rational multipliers $y$ with
\[
  \min_{v \in \text{box}}\; (y^{\mathsf T}A)\,v - y^{\mathsf T}b \;>\; 0 .
\]
The minimum of a linear function over a box is computed coordinatewise, in
exact arithmetic. But the true $v$ lies in the box and satisfies $Av \le b$,
so $(y^{\mathsf T}A)v \le y^{\mathsf T}b$: a contradiction. We call the
left side the \emph{gap} of case $k$. Each case uses $68$ to $130$
instances of Corollary~\ref{cor:J}, and the $60$ cases use $125126$
multiplier rows in all. Figure~\ref{fig:certificate} shows the gap of
every case. The smallest gap is in case $30$
($\ybar \in [0.1502, 0.1552]$):
\[
  \frac{42734354885632410509310622714769366500859}
       {13107200000000000000000000000000000000000000000}
  \;\approx\; 3.2604\cdot 10^{-6}.
\]
Since every case is contradictory and $\ybar$ lies in one of them, the
assumption $D \le (1/3+1/40)n$ with $n \ge 10^4$ is false. This proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. \qed

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

\paragraph{Exact verifier.} The multipliers were found by a floating-point
linear-programming solver and then rounded to rationals; the solver plays no
part in the proof. A separate verifier, written with the Python standard
library only and not importing the program that found the multipliers,
rebuilds every row from its tag, re-checks every side condition (grid
membership of each parameter, the chord covers of Lemma~\ref{lem:Cbox}, the
square-root bounds in Lemma~\ref{lem:Apex} and Corollary~\ref{cor:J}, the
cut of Lemma~\ref{lem:K}, the band edges and the case bounds), and
evaluates the box minimum in exact rational arithmetic for each of the $60$
cases. In two independent audits the check rejected mutated row tags and a
case with the rows of Corollary~\ref{cor:J} removed.

\paragraph{Lean.} The whole proof is formalized in Lean~4 with Mathlib. Each
row family is a Lean lemma about an arbitrary configuration in general
position under rational side conditions; a computable row builder maps a
tag to a rational row, checks the side conditions, and is proved sound. For
each of the $60$ cases a single theorem checks the weighted combination by
kernel reduction (\texttt{decide +kernel}), with the multipliers rounded
to integers at scale $2^{200}$. The final statement,
\texttt{Esgk.numDistances\_gt\_one\_third\_add\_kappa\_image\_gap}, says
that for an injective configuration of $n \ge 10000$ points in general
position, $(1/3 + 1/40)\,n < D$. Its axiom closure consists of the three standard
axioms \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and \texttt{Quot.sound};
the proof uses no \texttt{sorry} and no \texttt{native\_decide}.

\paragraph{Availability.} The certificate, the exact verifier and the Lean
sources are at
\url{https://github.com/mysticflounder/distinct-distances-general-position}.

\section{Remarks}\label{sec:remarks}

\paragraph{Limits of the method.} With the same row families, the
floating-point linear program has feasible points at $\delta = 1/39$ for
$\ybar \in \{0.128, 0.154, 0.180, 0.205\}$. This indicates (floating point
only; EMPIRICALLY VERIFIED in that sense) that these rows alone do not give
$\delta = 1/39$ by this kind of certificate. Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows
the feasible point at $\ybar = 0.154$: it is one profile that these inequalities do not exclude at $\delta = 1/39$ (the one that the solver returned; others can exist). Its pair distances concentrate in three narrow
ranges. At $\ybar = 0.154$, three further families of inequalities
(Bochner positivity of the distance measure, a lower bound for short pairs
from the area of the annulus, and a three-point covering inequality) each
cut the feasible point found but leave the linear program feasible.
HEURISTIC: a larger constant needs a further inequality of the kind of
Lemma~\ref{lem:J}.

\caption{A solution of the linear system at $\delta = 1/39$ and $\ybar = 0.154$, found by a floating-point solver; in floating point, the inequalities of this paper do not exclude it. (a)~The profile

\paragraph{Pinned distances.} Theorem~\ref{thm:main} bounds the total
number of distances. It gives no bound for the number $t(p)$ of distances
from a single point, and the questions of Erd\H{o}s and Pach whether
$\max_p t(p) \ge (1/3+\eps)n$ or $\sum_p t(p) \ge (1/3+\eps)n^2$ for sets
in general position \cite[Problem~2]{EP90}; I am not aware of an answer
to either. The second question is tied to the number $x_2$ of pairs with
two apexes. Let $c_k$ be the number of pairs $(p,r)$, $r > 0$, with
$d_r(p) = k$. As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:apex},
$2x_0 + x_1 = \sum_{\{a,b\}}(2 - \alpha) =
\sum_{p,r}(d - \binom d2)_{d = d_r(p)} = c_1 + c_2$, and
$\sum_p (3t(p) - (n-1)) = \sum_{p,r:\, d_r(p) \ge 1}(3 - d_r(p)) =
2c_1 + c_2$. Since $x_0 + x_1 \le 2x_0 + x_1 \le 2(x_0 + x_1)$ and
$c_1 + c_2 \le 2c_1 + c_2 \le 2(c_1 + c_2)$, the quantities $N - x_2$ and
$\sum_p (3t(p) - (n-1))$ are within a factor $4$ of each other. So a bound
$x_2 \le (1-c)N$ with $c > 0$ gives a positive answer to the second
question, and a positive answer gives such a bound. Erd\H{o}s and Purdy
\cite{EPu76} remark that, for sets in general position, they cannot even
show that the number of isosceles triangles is $o(n^2)$. The proof above
uses the common set $R$ of distances through \eqref{eq:Q} and does not
bound $x_2$.
